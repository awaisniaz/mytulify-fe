**NSC** (National Savings Certificate) and a bank **FD** (fixed deposit) are both lump-sum savings options that people often compare when they want predictable growth. They answer related questions — *If I park a fixed amount at a stated rate for a few years, what maturity might I see?* — but the product framing differs: NSC planning usually emphasizes **annual compounding to maturity**, while bank FDs often let you model **monthly, quarterly, or yearly compounding** (or even simple interest) depending on the product.

This article is educational planning content, not investment, tax, or legal advice. Scheme and bank rules, rates, limits, premature withdrawal, and tax treatment change — verify with official sources, your post office or bank, and a qualified advisor when decisions involve real money.

## Snapshot comparison

| Factor | NSC | FD |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Typical purpose | Post-office / government savings certificate growth | General bank deposit growth or locked savings |
| Deposit pattern often modeled | One-time lump sum | One-time lump sum |
| Interest feel | Compounded annually into maturity | Compounding frequency varies by bank product |
| Tenure feel | Commonly modeled around a multi-year block (often illustrated as 5 years) | Flexible tenures; product-specific |
| Planning tool | [NSC Calculator](/calculators/nsc-calculator) | [FD Calculator](/calculators/fd-calculator) |
| Key modeling difference | Year-by-year annual compounding table | Choose compounding frequency (or simple interest) |

Use the table as a lens for *your* maturity question — not as a ranking of which product is “better.”

## When NSC is the natural starting point

If you are modeling a **certificate-style lump sum** where interest **compounds once per year** into a single maturity figure — *If I buy ₹X of NSC at Y% for 5 years, what maturity and interest might I see?* — start with the [NSC Calculator](/calculators/nsc-calculator).

Practical steps:

1. Enter the certificate amount you can lock.
2. Enter the notified rate you want to assume.
3. Set tenure years (and optional extra months).
4. Read maturity, interest earned, effective yield, and the year-by-year table.

Deep dive on inputs and a worked example: [NSC Calculator Guide](/blog/nsc-calculator-guide).

## When an FD is the natural starting point

If you are modeling a **bank deposit** where you need to compare **monthly vs quarterly vs yearly compounding** (or simple interest) for the same principal — use the [FD Calculator](/calculators/fd-calculator). That tool answers a “how does compounding frequency change maturity?” question; NSC planning usually locks the compounding story to annual steps.

For yearly contribution-style government savings, compare with the [PPF Calculator](/calculators/ppf-calculator). For senior-citizen quarterly *payout* modeling, see the [SCSS Calculator](/calculators/scss-calculator).

## Same lump sum, different question

A useful way to decide which calculator to open first:

- Need **annual compounding** with a clear year-by-year certificate path? → [NSC Calculator](/calculators/nsc-calculator)
- Need to **toggle compounding frequency** on a bank deposit? → [FD Calculator](/calculators/fd-calculator)
- Need **yearly deposits** for a long block? → [PPF Calculator](/calculators/ppf-calculator)
- Need **quarterly interest paid out** while principal waits? → [SCSS Calculator](/calculators/scss-calculator)

Running both NSC and FD scenarios with the same principal and rate is often clarifying — even when eligibility or product rules differ — because it separates *certificate-style annual compounding* from *bank-product compounding choices*.

## Bottom line

NSC and FD calculators are complementary, not interchangeable. Use NSC when annual compounding into certificate maturity is the story; use FD when bank compounding frequency (or simple interest) is the story.
