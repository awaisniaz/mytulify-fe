Most job seekers either skip the cover letter entirely or send the same generic paragraph to every company. Both approaches waste a real opportunity: a tailored cover letter is often the only place you explain *why this role, why now* — not just that you exist on a resume.

This guide covers what actually works in 2026, plus how to generate a strong first draft with the free [AI Cover Letter Generator](/ai-tools/cover-letter-generator).

## Do you still need a cover letter?

Yes — when the application asks for one, or when you are switching careers, returning after a gap, or applying through a referral where a human reads everything first.

Skip it only when the posting explicitly says not to include one, or when the platform has no upload field (some ATS-only flows).

| Situation | Cover letter worth it? |
| --- | --- |
| Role asks for one | Always |
| Career change / pivot | Strong yes — explain the bridge |
| Referral or warm intro | Yes — reinforce the connection |
| High-volume "Easy Apply" only | Optional — focus on resume keywords |
| Internship / first job | Yes — show motivation and fit |

## What hiring managers scan in 30 seconds

Recruiters are not reading novels. They look for:

1. **Role and company named correctly** — typos here end the conversation.
2. **Two or three proof points** — achievements with numbers beat adjectives.
3. **Language that mirrors the job ad** — same terms for skills and responsibilities (helps ATS too).
4. **A reason you want *this* job** — not just any job.
5. **A clear next step** — "happy to discuss" beats "please consider my application."

Avoid opening with "I am writing to apply for…" — it wastes the most valuable line.

## Structure that works every time

```
Subject: [Job Title] — [Your Name]

Dear [Hiring Manager / Team Name],

Paragraph 1 — Hook: why this role + company (1–3 sentences)

Paragraph 2 — Proof: 2–3 achievements or skills mapped to the posting

Paragraph 3 — Close: enthusiasm + call to action

Best regards,
[Your Name]
```

Keep it to **250–400 words** unless the industry expects longer (academia, some government roles).

## ATS-friendly without sounding robotic

Applicant Tracking Systems score resumes and sometimes cover letters for keyword overlap with the job description. That does not mean stuffing every buzzword — it means using the same plain terms the posting uses for tools, seniority, and outcomes.

**Do:**

- Paste the real job ad into your drafting process (or into the AI tool).
- Match job title spelling exactly.
- Use standard section labels and plain text — no tables or text boxes.

**Don't:**

- Repeat the same keyword ten times.
- Lie about skills you do not have.
- Use images, icons, or fancy formatting in the letter file.

## Tone: match the company, not your mood

| Tone | Best for |
| --- | --- |
| Professional | Corporate, finance, healthcare, most B2B roles |
| Enthusiastic | Startups, marketing, customer-facing roles |
| Concise | Senior roles, busy hiring managers, repeat applicants |
| Career change | Pivoting industry or job function — explain transferable skills |

When in doubt, professional wins. You can always warm up one sentence if the company culture is clearly casual.

## Common mistakes

**One letter for every job.** Recruiters recognize templates instantly. At minimum, rewrite paragraph one and swap proof points per posting.

**Repeating your resume verbatim.** The letter should add context — why you care, how you think, what you will do in the first 90 days.

**Apologizing.** Never open with "Although I don't have all the requirements…" Lead with what you *do* bring.

**Being too long.** If you use the [Word Counter](/text-tools/word-counter) and you are past 450 words without a good reason, cut.
